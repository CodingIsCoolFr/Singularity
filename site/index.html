Voice, video and screen share use Discord's DAVE encryption, in a client built in C++ from the ground up. No Electron. No web view. Not a mod.

Measured, not claimed

Under a fifth of Discord's memory

Discord keeps six processes awake, and 1,012 MB that belong to it alone, just to stay open. Singularity is one process and 178 MB. Nearly six times the memory, about ten times the threads, and more than twice the disk, spent on the same job. This client does not drag a browser along to do it.

The average of two 60-second runs, sampled every second, on an Intel Core i9-13900K with 32 GB of RAM under Windows 11 Pro, 27 September 2026. Both apps were already open and neither was in a call. Singularity had an animated background chosen, but its own log recorded zero redraws a second while the samples ran, so the CPU here is the cost of sitting idle, not of playing that picture. With the picture actually moving, the 0.8.4 run was 0.60% against Discord's 0.23%. Memory is summed over every process each app runs. Private memory is the fairer figure: the working set counts memory Discord's six processes share once for each of them, and Windows had trimmed Singularity's working set while its window was not in front. An earlier run on 0.8.0 measured 639 MB and 107 threads; that version had a bug in how chat videos were played, fixed in 0.8.1. Calls and screen share have not been compared yet. The script and the raw results are in singularity-updates/benchmark.

In the client today

A full client, not a skin

Vencord and BetterDiscord sit on top of Discord. Singularity is the client. Voice, video, messages, friends and plugins are the program itself.

Talking

Voice channels you can actually join, with sound both ways, encrypted end to end the way Discord now requires. A channel with a user limit shows how full it is, and a full one will not let you in.

Mute, deafen, device and volume control, a speaking threshold, and a microphone test with a live level bar. Right-click someone in a call to turn just them up or down. That volume is Discord's, so the official app matches.

Calls run on a thread of their own, built the way Discord's own voice engine is: away from the window, with the same "Pro Audio" priority request to Windows. A busy window, a big server, or a game in the background no longer makes a twenty-person call chop.

Each voice gets a smart buffer modelled on WebRTC's NetEq, the one Discord's engine uses. It keeps packets in order, rebuilds a lost one from the backup copy Opus hides in the next, measures your line and picks its own cushion, and catches up a few milliseconds of voice at a time instead of dropping sound. On a simulated terrible connection it went from 74 clicks to none.

Sharing

Other people's cameras and shared screens arrive and are decoded above the conversation. In big calls the row under the stage scrolls instead of shrinking, and, like Discord, only the cameras on screen are downloaded.

You can share your own screen, and turn your own camera on. Encoding uses whatever hardware the machine has, and Cisco's software encoder when it has none.

Share a whole screen, or just one window, from the Screens and Windows tabs. A shared window keeps going out even when something else covers it.

A share can carry sound, the way Discord's does: everything on the computer except the call, or just one program, such as a browser or a game. It goes out marked as stream sound, so it never lights up a speaking ring.

Surround and virtual devices such as SteelSeries Sonar are taken in all their channels and mixed to stereo here, so a movie reaches your friends as loud as you hear it, not a quarter of that.

Pick which camera to use in Settings. A call that drops for a moment picks itself back up without leaving the channel.

Reading

History, live messages, and scrolling up through older ones. You can reply, edit, delete, attach a file, and react. Drag files onto the window or paste them to attach them. A welcome line shows the person's name as a clickable pill, not their number, and a channel link shows the channel name and takes you there, voice channels included.

An invite link gets a card under it, the way Discord draws one: the server's picture, who is online, and a Join button. The + under your servers joins one from a link or a code. When Discord asks for a captcha first, it appears for you to solve, as in the official app.

Images, GIFs, custom emoji from every server you are in, stickers and link previews. The picker has Emoji, Stickers and GIFs. Click any picture for a viewer with scroll to zoom and drag to pan.

Everyone

The member list follows the channel you have open. A voice channel lists who is in that call, and the list updates as people join and leave. Unread channels get a dot, mentions get a count.

New messages raise a Windows notification while the window is in the background, by the rules your Discord settings already set: every direct message, and in servers all messages, only @mentions or nothing, muted chats only when you are mentioned. Click one to open that chat. Unread direct messages sit under the home button as pictures with a red count, and Read All above them clears everything at once.

Set your own status from your name at the bottom: online, idle, do not disturb, invisible. Discord is told, and signing in picks up the status your account already has, so another device's choice is never overwritten. While the client is open, your profile shows a Playing card for Singularity, with the logo and a button to this site.

Everything else

Email and password sign-in with second factor, or scan a QR code with the Discord phone app to sign in with no password at all. Up to five accounts to switch between from the status menu. Server folders that sync with the official client: drag one server onto another to make a folder, and rename or recolour it. A Friends page with Online, All, Pending and Blocked, an Add Friend tab that sends a request by username, and an Active Now column. A voice card shows everyone in that call and joins it when clicked. A game card shows the artwork Discord sends with the activity. Opening Friends while you are in a call leaves the call connected and shows the list, not the stage.

Full profile windows with banners, badges, coloured role pills, notes, and mutual friends and servers. With Manage Roles you can give and take roles right there, and every Discord client sees the change.

Edit your own in Settings → Profiles, beside a live preview card that shows each change before you save it: avatar, display name, username, pronouns, about me, banner and its colour, the avatar decorations you own, and a nickname or avatar per server. Two-factor accounts are asked for their code, as in Discord.

One colour decides everything

The look

Pick a seed colour and the whole application is derived from it while it is running — surfaces, accents, every stylesheet token, and the accretion disk of the hole itself. No restart, no theme files. Try it: these retint this page and the background behind it, using the same rule the program uses.

Or put your own picture behind everything instead — an animated GIF, a WebP, a still image. There is a dim slider, and it is not decoration: every panel in the client is glass, so a bright picture makes the conversation hard to read.

Depth from layers
The rail, the sidebar and the conversation each sit a real step lighter than the one behind, so they read as separate planes without a single border drawn.
Colour is information
The status dots are the one thing the seed never touches. Green, yellow and red have to keep meaning online, away and busy whatever else changes.
Even the title bar
Drag it to a screen edge and it snaps to that half, that corner, or the whole screen. Drag it back out and it returns to the size it had. The menu along the top is still ours; the frame under it belongs to Windows, which is what remembers that size.
A hue-less seed
Black and grey have no hue, and scaling one would leave the disk unlit — so those take a silver branch instead. That is the default, and it is what you are looking at.

Privacy friendly

What stays on your machine

The tracking the official client sends was never written here. Your sign-in stays between you and Discord.

Your password
Sent to discord.com and nowhere else. Never written to disk. Never logged.
Your session
Sealed with your Windows account key. The log records its length, never its value.
Analytics events
The official client posts to an endpoint called /science as you click around. Singularity never calls it.
Read receipts
Nothing tells Discord which messages you have looked at, or when.
A real fingerprint
The client details sent on sign-in are fixed numbers written into the source, not your actual Windows version, locale or hardware — and they are identical for everyone running Singularity.
Session correlation
The fields the official client fills with identifiers linking your sessions together are sent empty.
Game detection
Nothing looks at what programs you have open.
Third party images
Pictures come only from Discord's own hosts.

What the Anonymous plugin genuinely does is the part Singularity does send and can stop sending. It strips tracking codes out of links before they leave. Because it rewrites what you say, it tests itself against six known cases every time it loads — and if any comes out wrong it refuses to touch messages for the rest of the session and says so. A tracking code getting through is a small harm. A broken link the sender cannot see is a worse one.

Why Nitro watch does not claim by itself

Discord does not catch automated accounts by measuring how fast you click. It catches them on the shape of the account: a redeem arriving from a session that never opened the channel, never scrolled, never moved a pointer, and that does it again next week at four in the morning. A random delay hides none of that, because none of it is about timing.

Dead codes are also posted deliberately, to see who bites. A person glances at a suspicious link and moves on. Anything redeeming on its own takes the bait every time, and marks itself doing it. So the part worth automating is the part that is slow for a person: noticing. The press stays yours, which is what keeps the account ordinary.

One setup program

Get it

It installs for you only and never asks for administrator. There is no service, no driver and nothing shared, so there is nothing to elevate for — and a small program asking for rights it does not need is how it starts looking untrustworthy.

There is no wizard either. Every page one would have shown asks something already decided by running it — where to put it, whether to continue, whether to start it afterwards. What you get is one dark window with the hole turning in it, and then the program.

Updating
The program looks for a newer version a few seconds after it starts, and quietly: an up to date copy says nothing at all. Agreeing does the whole thing — downloads, installs itself and starts again. No second dialog, no installer to click through, because by then there is nothing left to decide.
Uninstalling
Asks whether to remove your saved sign-in and settings, and defaults to keeping them. Those live in your profile rather than the install folder, so removing the program does not silently take the account with it.
Where the source is
Public, under the MIT license, at github.com/CodingIsCoolFr/Singularity. The installers stay in a separate repository, which is what the updater reads.
How it was built
